        &gt; You have been sent this message from a washingtonpost.com user as a<br>
        courtesy of the Washington Post (http://www.washingtonpost.com).<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; To view the entire article, go to<br>
        http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A37718-2001Nov15.html<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; On Left and Right, Concern Over Anti-Terrorism Moves<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; By George Lardner Jr.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; A growing chorus on the left and the right is accusing the Bush<br>
        administration of ignoring civil liberties while leaving the courts and<br>
        Congress out in the cold as it aggressively pursues the war on terrorism<br>
        here and abroad.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Critics ranging from the solidly liberal People for the American Way<br>
        Foundation to conservative Rep. Robert L. Barr Jr. (R-Ga.) are<br>
        characterizing recently announced administration plans as ethnic profiling,<br>
        power grabbing and overzealous law enforcement.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "Military tribunals, secret evidence, no numbers on how many people the<br>
        government is detaining," said Jim Zogby, president of the Arab-American<br>
        Institute. "We're looking like a Third World country."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; The latest focus of the debate is an order signed by President Bush this<br>
        week that empowers him to order military trials here and abroad for<br>
        international terrorists and their collaborators.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; But other complaints concern Attorney General John D. Ashcroft's decision<br>
        to monitor conversations between lawyers and some clients in federal custody<br>
        if Ashcroft believes it is necessary to thwart future terrorism; the plan to<br>
        question 5,000 foreign nationals who recently entered the country; and the<br>
        FBI's visits to hundreds of college campuses to check on the records of<br>
        foreign students, mostly from Middle Eastern countries.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Under pressure from the Justice Department, the State Department also has<br>
        agreed to slow temporarily the granting of visas to Arab and Muslim males,<br>
        ages 16 to 45, from 25 countries so the FBI and INS can conduct security<br>
        checks.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; The administration has made no apologies, saying the nation is in the<br>
        midst of an extraordinary emergency.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "I think it's important to understand that we are at war now," Ashcroft<br>
        said earlier this week in defending military tribunals.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Assistant Attorney General Viet Dinh said that the overriding goal of the<br>
        Justice Department is to "prevent further terrorist attacks" but that at the<br>
        same time it must take care "not to redefine the line between law<br>
        enforcement and civil liberties."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Critics in Congress, legal scholars and spokesmen for the nation's Arab<br>
        American community have voiced misgivings about the new anti-terrorism laws,<br>
        passed last month as the USA-Patriot Act. But they are far more vocal about<br>
        what the administration has done since then.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Patrick J. Leahy (D-Vt.) said he<br>
        intends to hold hearings on the military tribunals directive and other<br>
        recent steps that have been taken without consulting Congress.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "There's a lot of disquiet among both Republican and Democratic senators<br>
        who think the rules of law are being turned on their head, and they wonder<br>
        what we gain by it," Leahy said in an interview. "I want to know what<br>
        security needs are being met, or whether this is action for the sake of<br>
        having action when you can't catch people."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; House Judiciary Committee members have also called for hearings. Barr said<br>
        the administration should have given the new anti-terrorist laws time to<br>
        work, then gone back to Congress if they turned out to be insufficient.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "Instead, it seems their attitude is, 'Well, that wasn't enough so we're<br>
        going to take more,' " Barr told a reporter. "I'm not sure we can ever<br>
        satisfy the federal government's insatiable appetite for more power."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Advocacy groups from both sides of the political spectrum have joined the<br>
        debate. Ralph Neas, president of the People for the American Way Foundation,<br>
        accused Ashcroft of "waging a relentless assault on civil liberties." Among<br>
        the most troubling actions, Neas said, was the order empowering Ashcroft to<br>
        violate the attorney-client privilege without a court order.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; The Justice Department said the order so far pertains only to 13 people in<br>
        federal custody, none of them connected to the Sept. 11 attacks.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "Terrorism isn't the only threat to our way of life," Neas said yesterday.<br>
        "We need an attorney general who will stand up to terrorists, but we also<br>
        need an attorney general who will stand up for the Constitution and the Bill<br>
        of Rights."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Zogby said the administration's emergency measures are already undermining<br>
        U.S. credibility abroad, where thousands of Arab men, students, businessmen<br>
        and, in some cases, royalty are having visa requests held up for security<br>
        checks.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Just back from a trip to Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, Zogby<br>
        said the fear of U.S. authorities there is widespread.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "Forty percent of the students from the UAE who were in the United States<br>
        . . . have already gone home," Zogby said.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Tim Lynch, director of the Cato Institute's project on criminal justice,<br>
        said it appears that the president's strong support in public opinion polls<br>
        has fostered "an arrogance at the White House." He said officials believe<br>
        they can take presidential power "farther than it's gone before."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Lynch was especially critical of the order for military tribunals. They<br>
        would be able to impose sentences as severe as death on a two-thirds vote,<br>
        hold trials in secret and rely on evidence that would be rejected in a civil<br>
        court.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "It undermines the courts, obviously," Lynch said of the order, "and it<br>
        undermines Congress because it is essentially legislating action by<br>
        presidential edict."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Issued by Bush as commander in chief, the military order directs the<br>
        secretary of defense to detain indefinitely any noncitizen who Bush has<br>
        "reason to believe" is a past or present member of Osama bin Laden's al<br>
        Qaeda terror network, has engaged in international terrorism directed at<br>
        U.S. interests or has "knowingly harbored such individuals."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Military tribunals, using whatever standard of proof Defense Secretary<br>
        Donald H. Rumsfeld chooses, then would be able to try those individuals for<br>
        "all offenses triable by military commission" -- in other words, offenses<br>
        under the laws of war.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "This is absolutely, totally constitutional," an administration official<br>
        conversant with the decree said yesterday. "The only ones to be tried will<br>
        be foreign enemy belligerents."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; That could include an al Qaeda cell member planning more acts of violence,<br>
        he said. "If they're hiding and planning acts of violence," he said, "they<br>
        are in violation of the laws of war. The U.S. Constitution doesn't protect<br>
        them."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Bush's order does not allow for judicial review. Several legal experts<br>
        said a little-noticed provision at the end of the directive order also<br>
        appears to be an effort by the president to suspend the right of habeas<br>
        corpus, which prisoners can use to challenge their detention.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; President Franklin D. Roosevelt claimed such authority, without success,<br>
        when he ordered a secret military trial of eight Nazi saboteurs during World<br>
        War II.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Bush said that "any individual subject to this order shall not be<br>
        privileged to seek any remedy . . . in any court of the United States, or<br>
        any state thereof."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "The word 'privileged' is the tip-off," said Philip A. Lacovara, a former<br>
        deputy U.S. solicitor general. He said he was surprised by the provision<br>
        even though he favors military tribunals as the best response to the attacks<br>
        of Sept. 11.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; "The Constitution sets out only two grounds for suspending the privilege<br>
        of challenging one's detention on a habeas corpus petition: one is invasion<br>
        and the other is rebellion. Even in the Civil War, the courts were reluctant<br>
        to allow President Lincoln to dispense with habeas corpus."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Lacovara added: "It adds another level of controversy to the order."<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; Dan Bartlett, the White House's communications director, denied that the<br>
        order forecloses habeas corpus petitions for noncitizens detained in the<br>
        United States for military trials.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; During World War II, the Supreme Court reviewed the saboteurs' case<br>
        despite Roosevelt's attempts to block their petition for release. The White<br>
        House is aware that would probably occur if Bush tried the same thing,<br>
        administration officials said.<br>
        &gt;<br>
        &gt; <em>Staff writers Dan Eggen and Mary Beth Sheridan contributed to this<br>
        report.</em><br>
